# Multiplayer scaling: a world of 25, 50, 100 or 1,000 players

A research report of 2026-11-07 on what it would take to raise the most players in a world from 8
to 25, 50, 100 and 1,000: an audit of the multiplayer code with measurements, and research on
netcode, server topology, browser stacks and phone crowds, hosting and cost, and the game's design
in a shared world. Its finding in a line: lockstep stops near 25; copies reach 1,000.

The plan to implement its stages 0 to 6 is [multiplayer_scaling_plan.md](multiplayer_scaling_plan.md).

Moving Pellagos past 8 players is a three-line change, but the lockstep design behind the cap gives out quickly: every client re-simulates every occupied region of the 8 km world and receives every player's commands, so each client's CPU and download grow with the player count and the authority's upload grows with its square. Measured for this audit on a 4-core machine, **25 players spread across the map cost each joined desktop 9.1 ms of every 50 ms step**, about 45 ms on a phone at the code's own 5× phone ratio, and **100 spread players push the authority's mean step to 60 ms, its upload to 45 Mbit/s and its welcome snapshot to 4.5 MB**, past the relay's 4 MB message cap. So 25 is the last tier lockstep reaches, and only for desktops or for players who stay together. 50 and 100 need what every shipped game of that size uses: a server-authoritative world that sends each client only what is near it (interest management, binary deltas, prediction of the player's own hero), plus a fix for the authority's players-times-entities passes, dungeons and interiors run as instances on their own threads, and other players drawn as crowd figures. One 8 km world holding 1,000 players would average 15.6 per km², denser than a full Rust server, and would need about 150 ms of authority work per 50 ms step even at the best per-player cost measured; every game at that scale splits its population into zones or copies. Hosting is the cheapest part of all this: **about $40–200 a month on Hetzner or OVH for a 25–100-player world and €200–400 for 1,000 split into copies**, against roughly $4,900 a month on AWS EC2 once internet egress is paid. The owner should build server-authoritative replication on the existing world server for **worlds of 50–100 players**, reach 1,000 as **about ten such worlds behind one directory and shared account, chat and market services**, keep today's lockstep for private 8-player worlds, and not attempt one seamless 1,000-player world.

## The cap is three copies of 8; the ceiling is lockstep

The limit itself is easy to move. It lives in three hand-kept places: `MOST_PLAYERS = 8` in the netcode ([netplay.js:117](../client/js/core/netplay.js#L117)), the world server's `mostPlayers` setting bounded at `max: 8` ([worlds.js:105](../server/storage/worlds.js#L105)), and the "Host on a Server" select offering 8 down to 1 ([main.js:4344](../client/js/main.js#L4344)). The design note's only stated reason is that "encounter scaling, bandwidth and snapshots are tuned for it" ([world_server_plan.md:60](../generated/world_server_plan.md#L60)). Nothing in the wire format or the id scheme depends on 8: joiners get unbounded `guest-n` names and the motion stream indexes actors with a uint16, room for 65,536 ([netplay.js:430](../client/js/core/netplay.js#L430), [motion.js:59](../client/js/core/motion.js#L59)). The next walls stand close behind it. Relay and broker rooms admit **15 joiners** ([relay.js:48](../server/relay.js#L48), [broker.js:79](../server/broker.js#L79)); a world server admits **64 players across all its worlds** ([limits.js:22](../server/access/limits.js#L22)); a relayed message may not exceed **4 MB** ([relay.js:48](../server/relay.js#L48)); parties hold **4**, with a four-colour palette to match ([host.js:369](../client/js/core/host.js#L369), [mapicons.js:586](../client/js/app/mapicons.js#L586)); and a unit test fills a world to `MOST_PLAYERS` and expects the next hello to be refused ([netplay.test.js:759-765](../test/netplay.test.js#L759-L765)).

The network model is what actually limits a world. A Pellagos world is a deterministic lockstep simulation with one authority: either the hosting player's browser, or one worker thread per world on the world server running the identical `Host` and `Hosting` code ([world-worker.js:41-46](../server/world-worker.js#L41-L46), [world-thread.js:1-3](../server/world-thread.js#L1-L3)). The authority records every step, command, join and found path as an op, and every 50 ms it flushes the same JSON text to every player ([netplay.js:479-545](../client/js/core/netplay.js#L479-L545), [runner.js:14-18](../client/js/core/runner.js#L14-L18)). Each joiner replays the whole world step for step on its own copy. Every 5 s that copy is checked against a checksum hashing every actor's id, position, health, death and target, plus gold, packs, trades and shop stock ([host.js:3329-3380](../client/js/core/host.js#L3329-L3380)). A 5 Hz "motion" packet, holding the 64 actors nearest *any* player and identical for everyone, exists only to catch a copy that has gone astray ([motion.js:1-6](../client/js/core/motion.js#L1-L6), [motion.js:49-80](../client/js/core/motion.js#L49-L80)). Nothing is filtered per recipient and nothing is compressed: messages are `JSON.stringify` text, with binary sent as base64 because "the relay carries text only" ([wire.js:1-24](../client/js/core/wire.js#L1-L24)). The authority does simulate only around players: building insides open within 22 m, town soldiers wake within 120 m, and about four wild creatures are kept within 60 m of each group ([host.js:82-119](../client/js/core/host.js#L82-L119), [host.js:400](../client/js/core/host.js#L400)). But it simulates *every* player's bubble on one thread, and so must every client.

### Measured: spread-out players, not player count, break the budget

For this audit a scratch harness modelled on the repository's `step-budget` script ([step-budget.js](../scripts/step-budget.js)) did the following on a 4-core Node 22 machine: built seed 2's world, joined N scripted players to a hostless `Host` as a world server does, and ran 30 s of world time after a 10 s warm-up. It counted the bytes sent to N fake peers and replayed one peer's stream on a joined copy in the same process. The harness and its raw results were kept only in that session's scratchpad, not in the repository. "Spread" puts each player outside a different settlement across the 8 km map; "one town" puts them all in the human start town. Players walked to a random point within 40 m every 5 s and did not fight, except in the stick-input run, where every player sent a held-stick order every 500 ms as `steering.js` does ([steering.js:33-45](../client/js/app/steering.js#L33-L45)).

**Occupied regions, not players, drive the cost.** A hundred players in one town cost the authority 2.5 ms a step, less than 25 players in 25 places (4.2 ms mean, 35.6 ms p99), because each spread player brings about 17 simulated actors with them: 1,675 at 100 spread, of which 667 are townsfolk, 450 soldiers and 457 beasts. The cost per spread player also climbs, from 0.15 ms at 8 players to 0.17, 0.28 and 0.60 ms at 25, 50 and 100. That is the signature of passes that scale with players times entities. `groupsOf` is O(players²) ([strength.js:162-191](../client/js/core/strength.js#L162-L191)); `#wilds` measures every wild creature against every player every 500 ms ([host.js:9525-9605](../client/js/core/host.js#L9525-L9605)); and `packMotion` loops every actor against every player ([motion.js:53-75](../client/js/core/motion.js#L53-L75)). All three run even though the battle already has an 8 m spatial index for its own queries ([battle.js:660-665](../client/js/core/battle.js#L660-L665)). Bandwidth grows the same way. Ops cost every client about 0.22 KB/s per actively steering player and 0.45 KB/s per spread player, so each client's download is O(N) and the authority's upload, N times that, is O(N²).

**The binding constraint is the client.** The code puts a phone at about five times a desktop's step cost: "a step with a fight in it takes some 2 ms on a desktop and 10 on a phone" ([netplay.js:125](../client/js/core/netplay.js#L125)). On that ratio a phone already spends about 20 ms of every 50 ms step replaying the world with 8 spread players, and about 45 ms with 25 (estimate). That replay shares the page's main thread with drawing the frame ([game.js:3727-3760](../client/js/app/game.js#L3727-L3760)). A copy that falls 100 steps (5 s) behind asks for a whole new snapshot ([netplay.js:129](../client/js/core/netplay.js#L129), [netplay.js:176](../client/js/core/netplay.js#L176)), 1.2 MB at 25 spread, and then falls behind again. At 100 spread even a desktop's first replay step took 5.6 s, because the copy builds terrain round every player, so a joiner would be past the 5 s limit before it began. Memory follows the same shape: the process holding the authority and one copy reached 585, 707 and 825 MB resident at 25, 50 and 100 spread, against 230–290 MB for 8–100 players in one town. **The 8-player cap protects phones more than it protects the server.**

These figures flatter the design. The runs had no combat. The repository's own two-hour soak, with 8 players and a field battle every four minutes, recorded a worst-minute p99 of 30.4 ms, with the battle swelling to 260–580 actors ([world_server_plan.md:1460-1470](../generated/world_server_plan.md#L1460-L1470)). Invasions bring 120 raiders to each occupied town every 30–60 minutes ([invasions.js:62-74](../client/js/core/invasions.js#L62-L74)). Field battles run up to 200 a side ([host.js:651](../client/js/core/host.js#L651)). A war turn spends about 30 ms making chunks on the same thread once a minute ([WAR.md:3084-3088](../docs/WAR.md#L3084-L3088)). One machine ran the authority, its terrain workers and the copy together, and at 50 and 100 spread it could not keep real time: 30 s of world time took 45 s and 136 s of wall time. No phone was measured and no network latency was emulated. Every breakpoint below therefore arrives earlier in real play, not later. The repository's written budgets are stale too: they allow "about 1.2 KB/s motion and 0.5 KB/s ops" per player ([world_server_plan.md:1530](../generated/world_server_plan.md#L1530)) against the 7.4–57 KB/s measured, and they estimate "some 120 KB" for a snapshot ([netplay.js:124-127](../client/js/core/netplay.js#L124-L127)) against the 0.19–4.5 MB measured.

The deeper problem is that lockstep cannot accept interest management. "Every copy simulates everything" is the contract that makes lockstep cheap at 8 players, since clients receive only commands, and it is also why lockstep cannot grow: any per-client filtering breaks the checksums and the replay. Past about 25 players, clients must stop re-simulating the world and start rendering a server's view of it. The authority already has the right shape for that. The world server runs the same `Host` in a worker per world, has no host player ([world-worker.js:112-119](../server/world-worker.js#L112-L119)), and already keeps worlds and heroes on disk. What has to change is what the server sends and what the client does with it.

## Shipped games bound every client's cost; Pellagos bounds none

### Interest management and deltas hold a player near 20–30 KB/s

If every client receives every entity, each client downloads (N−1) × rate × bytes per entity and the server sends N times that. At a compact 16 bytes per entity and 20 Hz, that comes to 7.7 KB/s per client at 25 players, 32 KB/s at 100 and 320 KB/s at 1,000, where the server would need about 2.6 Gbit/s before counting a single NPC (arithmetic). Production netcode caps the per-client cost rather than paying it. Glenn Fiedler's reference design targets **256 kbit/s (32 KB/s)** and fills each packet with at most 64 state updates, chosen by a *priority accumulator*: unsent objects keep their accumulated priority until they win a slot ([Gaffer on Games: State Synchronization](https://gafferongames.com/post/state_synchronization/)). Quantisation and delta coding against the last acknowledged state cut an entity update from about 40 bytes to **about 7 bytes per changed entity** ([Gaffer on Games: Snapshot Compression](https://gafferongames.com/post/snapshot_compression/)). Sending discrete changes as events rather than diffed properties gave Riot "100x to 10000x" improvements in many cases ([Riot: VALORANT's 128-Tick Servers](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/valorants-128-tick-servers)).

Interest management decides who receives what. Albion Online tracks objects in a grid of **10 m cells** and subscribes each client to whatever enters an interest area that moves with the player ([Albion slides, 2016](https://es.slideshare.net/davidsalz54/albion-online-software-architecture-of-an-mmo-talk-at-quo-vadis-2016-berlin)). Photon Fusion turns interest shapes into 16–32 m cells, trading traffic against CPU ([Photon Fusion 2 interest management](https://doc.photonengine.com/fusion/current/addons/interest-management/architecture)). Unreal's Iris uses a sphere prioritiser that gives full priority inside 10 m, 0.2 at 50 m and 0.1 beyond, accumulating until each object is sent ([Iris prioritization](https://dev.epicgames.com/documentation/en-us/unreal-engine/iris-prioritization-in-unreal-engine)). The effect is that near objects go every tick, mid-range ones about every fifth and far ones every tenth. Unreal's Replication Graph exists because per-actor, per-connection relevancy checks did not scale to Fortnite's **100 players and about 50,000 replicated actors** ([UE 4.27 Replication Graph](https://docs.unrealengine.com/4.27/InteractiveExperiences/Networking/ReplicationGraph)). Interest management is also cheat resistance, because a client cannot leak what it was never sent ([Mirror: Interest Management](https://mirror-networking.gitbook.io/docs/manual/interest-management)).

Pellagos already owns every ingredient. It has 64 m terrain chunks ([overworld.js:1-3](../client/js/core/overworld.js#L1-L3)), relevance radii for what is alive near players ([host.js:82-130](../client/js/core/host.js#L82-L130)), the battle's 8 m grid, and motion's 160 m reach with its 64-actor cap ([motion.js:27](../client/js/core/motion.js#L27)). A per-client view could be the actors in the 3 × 3 chunks round each player (a 192 m square), fed through a priority accumulator to at most about 64 actors per packet. At about 10 bytes per quantised update that costs 13 KB/s for 64 actors at 20 Hz and 30 KB/s for 150, whatever the world's population (arithmetic).

### Twenty steps a second is right; how Pellagos degrades under load is wrong

Pellagos steps at 20 Hz ([battle.js:96](../client/js/core/battle.js#L96)), the mainstream rate for this kind of game. Source-engine games were tuned for 20 updates a second, with clients rendering 100 ms (two updates) behind ([Valve Source Multiplayer Networking, mirror](https://gist.github.com/CoolOppo/fe0586836de3fb2f90f9)). New World simulates at 30 Hz, against "around five times per second" for traditional MMOs ([AWS for Games](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/gametech/the-unique-architecture-behind-amazon-games-seamless-mmo-new-world)). Old School RuneScape ticks every 0.6 s ([OSRS wiki: Tick](https://oldschool.runescape.wiki/w/Tick)). The established levers for scale are to keep the simulation rate, send far entities less often, and run expensive passes every Nth tick. Riot cut server-side animation cost by about 75% by computing it every fourth frame ([Riot](https://www.riotgames.com/en/news/valorants-128-tick-servers)), and Pellagos already runs its relevance passes every 500 ms ([host.js:87](../client/js/core/host.js#L87)).

The weak point is degradation. Under overload, EVE Online slows a star system's game time to as little as 10% rather than break consistency ([EVE University: Time Dilation](https://wiki.eveuniversity.org/Time_Dilation)), and Overwatch drops clients without the bandwidth back to its lower update rate ([PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/overwatchs-high-bandwidth-update-is-live-heres-a-rundown-of-what-it-means)). Pellagos's degradation is the most expensive available: a lagging client is sent a whole snapshot, encoded on the authority's step thread (118 ms at 100 spread) and sent separately to each requester ([netplay.js:885-918](../client/js/core/netplay.js#L885-L918)). Under state replication, a slow phone would simply receive fewer far updates.

### Dedicated servers, WebSockets and shared JavaScript rules

Peer hosting does not scale. Full-mesh WebRTC tops out at about 4–12 participants on consumer links because each peer uploads to every other ([BlogGeek.me](https://bloggeek.me/webrtc-p2p-mesh/)), and every production example here above about 25–50 players uses a dedicated authoritative server with client-side prediction. Albion, for example, "predicts that action on the client while sending updates to the authoritative server" ([Unity: Architecting Albion Online](https://unity.com/blog/albion-online-cross-platform-pvp-mmo-architecture)). Pellagos's browser host simulates the whole world on its page's main thread and stops the world for everyone when a hosting phone's page is hidden ([netplay.js:146-154](../client/js/core/netplay.js#L146-L154)). At 25 spread players it would have to upload 4 Mbit/s, and through the relay it would hit the 15-joiner room cap. Shared worlds above 8 players therefore belong on the world server.

The server should stay in JavaScript. On one core, Node 26 with the `ws` package handled **45,000 four-message WebSocket round trips a second**, against 49,000 with uWebSockets.js ([Lemire, Oct 2026](https://lemire.me/blog/2026/10/10/websocket-servers-in-javascript-node-js-bun-and-deno-in-2026/)), so the socket layer is not the bottleneck. The real costs are building a different view for each client and garbage collection. One Node game server ticking at 100 Hz showed 16–50 ms gaps between frames that GC logs understated ([v8-users](https://groups.google.com/g/v8-users/c/tunDe2yVUoQ)), and one developer's TypeScript match handler for 200 clients at 20 Hz hit "too slow" warnings ([Heroic Labs forum](https://forum.heroiclabs.com/t/scaling-authoritative-matches-for-200-players-with-high-tick-rate-best-practices-and-configuration-tips/5804)). Figma's answer to the same pressure was to keep Node for networking and move only the GC-sensitive core into a Rust child process ([Rust in production at Figma](https://madebyevan.com/figma/rust-in-production-at-figma/)). Pellagos's game rules are shared by server and client, and that shared code is what will let a client predict its own hero. Rewriting the rules in another language would split them in two. Once the server is authoritative, determinism is no longer required. If profiling demands it, a hot kernel such as the spatial grid or snapshot encoding can move to WebAssembly, the route Pellagos already takes with Recast.

WebSocket over TLS is enough to about 100 players per world. agar.io ran on it ([Gaffer on Games, 2017](https://gafferongames.com/post/why_cant_i_send_udp_packets_from_a_browser/)), and its cost is TCP head-of-line blocking, which a 100 ms interpolation buffer and a "send the newest state, never queue stale ones" rule absorb. WebTransport became Baseline when Safari 26.4 shipped it in March 2026 ([WebKit](https://webkit.org/blog/17862/webkit-features-for-safari-26-4/)) and reaches 92.37% of users ([caniuse](https://caniuse.com/webtransport)). However, a WebKit bug reports its unreliable datagrams missing on iOS 26.6 ([GitHub research issue citing WebKit 322201](https://github.com/djosh34/t3-optimizations/issues/2)). There is also a decisive cost argument: Cloudflare proxies WebSockets on every plan, including Free, with unmetered L3–L7 DDoS protection ([Cloudflare WebSockets](https://developers.cloudflare.com/network/websockets/), [Cloudflare DDoS](https://developers.cloudflare.com/ddos-protection/)), whereas arbitrary UDP needs Spectrum on Enterprise ([Cloudflare Spectrum docs preview](https://codex-container-api-docs.previews.developers.cloudflare.com/spectrum/protocols-per-plan/index.md)). Pellagos should send binary frames over WebSocket first and treat WebTransport as a later, optional path.

### Every 1,000-player world is split into zones or copies

Games whose single process simulates a whole persistent, editable world cap out low: Valheim at 10 ([Winternode](https://winternode.com/help/games/valheim/configuration/player-limit)), Enshrouded at 16 ([XGamingServer](https://xgamingserver.com/blog/enshrouded-co-op-multiplayer-player-count/)), Palworld at 32 ([Shockbyte](https://shockbyte.com/ru/blog/how-to-play-palworld-with-more-than-4-and-more-than-32-players)). Rust reaches about 100–300 on very fast single cores, with "entity count … the dominant tax on the single-threaded simulation" ([GameServerKings](https://www.gameserverkings.com/knowledge-base/rust/server-performance.md)). Minecraft's Folia splits a world into regions ticking in parallel and peaked at about 330 players, helps only when players are spread out, and set plugin-compatibility expectations "at zero" ([PaperMC/Folia](https://github.com/PaperMC/Folia)). Pellagos's one-world-per-thread server sits squarely in this band.

Larger worlds get there by coarse ticks, by spatial partitioning or by copies. Old School RuneScape fits 2,000 players into a world by ticking every 0.6 s ([OSRS: More Worlds, More Power](https://oldschool.runescape.wiki/w/Update:More_Worlds,_More_Power:_The_Road_To_Greater_Capacity)). New World runs about 2,500 players per world at 30 Hz across a hub of seven EC2 C5.9xlarge machines, each owning two grid subdivisions, handing players from machine to machine as they move and writing about 800,000 records to DynamoDB every 30 s ([AWS for Games](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/gametech/the-unique-architecture-behind-amazon-games-seamless-mmo-new-world)). EVE runs one node per busy star system, about 13 nodes per 16-core machine ([CCP: Tranquility Tech IV](https://www.eveonline.com/news/view/tranquility-tech-iv)). Albion splits its world into clusters of about 1 km², each single-threaded, and hands players between them *through the database* even when both clusters sit on the same server ([GameDev.net summary of Albion's architecture](https://gamedev.net/news/3258-architecting-albion-online-how-sandbox-interactive-built-a-pvp-mmo-to-scale/)). BitCraft independently arrived at the same shape: region databases, "each … a single-threaded actor", under a global root ([SpacetimeDB: does it scale?](https://spacetimedb.com/blog/how-does-spacetime-scale)). The common pattern is **one single-threaded simulation per zone, plus a shared database and global services**.

Seamless multi-server worlds have a poor record. Worlds Adrift shut in 2019 and could not be hosted outside SpatialOS ([Wikipedia: Worlds Adrift](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Worlds_Adrift)), and the 1,000-player Mavericks was cancelled with early builds handling about 100 ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/the-1000-player-battle-royale-game-mavericks-proving-grounds-is-canceled)). Star Citizen shipped only static server meshing, at about 500 players a shard, in December 2024 ([Star Citizen Wiki](https://starcitizen.tools/StarNetwork)), and was still building "quasi-dynamic" meshing in 2026 ([StarShipDealers summary](https://www.starshipdealers.com/blog/sc-live-tech-talk-server-meshing-2026/)). Copies, by contrast, work. A Blizzard developer called WoW's layering "just sharding with a lot of sticky tape" ([PC Gamer](https://pcgamer.com/the-server-tech-that-saved-wow-10-years-ago-is-causing-problems-for-classics-season-of-discovery-dev-reveals-a-layer-is-actually-just-sharding-with-a-lot-of-sticky-tape)). Guild Wars 2's megaserver opens and closes map copies and places players by party, guild and language; in testing it raised average population per map copy by 225% ([ArenaNet: Megaserver](https://www.guildwars2.com/en/news/introducing-the-megaserver-system/)).

Persistence is where partitioned worlds bleed. New World's November 2021 gold duplication, tied to lag at trading posts, forced Amazon to switch off every wealth transfer in the game ([PC Gamer](https://www.pcgamer.com/amazon-disables-new-world-wealth-transfers-to-fight-gold-dupe-exploit)). The standard defences are three: one owner per character at a time (Albion's handoff through the database), both sides of a trade in one transaction, and holding a departing player's session open until the next save lands ([Roblox DevForum](https://devforum.roblox.com/t/economydatastore-duplication-prevention/637443)). Pellagos already has the partition boundaries but not the persistence. Dungeon levels are separate maps placed far from everything else, from x = 20,000 ([DUNGEONS.md:749-755](../docs/DUNGEONS.md#L749-L755)), which makes them instances waiting to happen. But a world is saved as one whole gzipped JSON snapshot at every war turn, taken on the world thread, and heroes are written as JSON files when they leave ([keeper.js:1-6](../server/keeper.js#L1-L6), [worlds.js:1-20](../server/storage/worlds.js#L1-L20), [world-worker.js:145-158](../server/world-worker.js#L145-L158)). There is no database.

### Phones draw a dozen full characters, not a hundred

Shipped MMOs cap what they draw. Guild Wars 2's Character Model Limit sets how many characters appear at all and draws the rest of a crowd as generic models, while still showing every name plate ([GW2 Wiki: Options](https://wiki.guildwars2.com/wiki/Options)). In Three.js, a laptop falls below 60 fps beyond about 200 independently skinned meshes, but sharing skeletons between identically animated characters reached 1,000 at 60 fps ([three.js forum #58196](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/optimization-of-large-amounts-100-1000-of-skinned-meshes-cpu-bottlenecks/58196)). Vertex-animation-texture instancing draws 500 animated robots in three draw calls ([three-vat](https://github.com/MikeFernandez-Pro/three-vat)). Community budgets put phones at roughly 100–200 draw calls a frame ([abratabia](https://abratabia.com/mobile-browser-performance/reducing-draw-calls.php)). Safari tends to hold iPhones to 60 fps ([WebKit bug 294338](https://www2.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=294338)), and it reloads a tab at around a 1.5 GB soft memory limit ([WebKit bug 277848](https://bugs.webkit.org/show_bug.cgi?id=277848)). Pellagos draws every other player as a full, unmerged MakeHuman of **22–38 draw calls and 80–100 thousand triangles**, with its own skin texture ([CHARACTERS.md:2325-2332](../docs/CHARACTERS.md#L2325-L2332)). Players are never eligible for the crowd figures that townsfolk, soldiers and followers use ([game.js:2575-2577](../client/js/app/game.js#L2575-L2577), [throng.js:1-8](../client/js/app/throng.js#L1-L8)). Their name plates are DOM elements ([hud.js:368](../client/js/app/hud.js#L368)), and DOM-style labels lag at around 300 ([three.js forum #66927](https://discourse.threejs.org/t/how-to-create-lots-of-optimized-2d-text-labels/66927)). Twenty-five players gathered in a town would cost 550–950 draw calls and a hundred would cost 2,200–3,800, roughly 5–20 times a phone's whole budget.

The budgets Pellagos should design to above 25 players follow from all this:

| Resource | Budget | Pellagos today at 50 spread | Basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| Download per client | ≤ 20–30 KB/s, flat as N grows | 33 KB/s, rising with N | [Gaffer's 32 KB/s target](https://gafferongames.com/post/state_synchronization/) |
| Authority step at 20 Hz | mean ≤ ~25 ms, p99 ≤ 50 ms | 13.8 / 68.5 ms | [world_server_plan.md:1527](../generated/world_server_plan.md#L1527) sets p99 ≤ 50 ms |
| Authority time per player | ≤ ~150–250 µs per step | 280 µs, rising | a 25 ms mean shared by 100–150 players (arithmetic) |
| Client world simulation | its own hero only | 19.2 ms per step on a desktop | lockstep replay removed |
| Welcome on joining | ≤ 350 KB within 2 s | 2.33 MB | [world_server_plan.md:1531](../generated/world_server_plan.md#L1531) |

## 25 bends lockstep, 50 and 100 break it, 1,000 needs copies

| Tier | What breaks first today | Architecture it needs | Game-design changes | Hosting a month (bare metal / AWS EC2) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25 | Phones replaying 25 spread regions (~45 ms per 50 ms step, estimate); 15-joiner rooms; authority p99 35.6 ms before any combat | Lockstep on the world server only; authority passes fixed; welcome throttle | Per-party dungeon instances; shared tagging and personal loot; rest, pause and AFK rules; rare encounters per group; area chat and reporting | €0–36 / ≈$225 |
| 50 | Authority p99 68.5 ms; desktop replay 19 ms per step; the server-wide 64-player cap | Server-authoritative replication with per-client interest management, binary deltas and own-hero prediction | Area directors; contribution tiers; squads above the party of 4; guilds; a market with a fee | €36–79 / ≈$341 |
| 100 | Authority mean 60 ms; 4.5 MB snapshot; 45 Mbit/s upload; joiners 5.6 s behind | Replication plus instances and regions on their own threads; per-character database; players drawn as crowd figures | Bosses scaling to ~100; loot lockouts; caps and overflow copies for interiors; moderation; economy tracking | €79–159 / ≈$575 |
| 1,000 | Everything, by an order of magnitude; 15.6 players per km² | About ten 100-player worlds behind a directory and shared account, chat, friends and market services; separate database | Placement by party, friends and guild; merging; queues; layer-hop rules; once-per-world content per copy | €200–400 / ≈$4,890 |

### 25 players: reachable on lockstep for desktops, not for phones that spread out

Twenty-five is the one tier the current design can reach, with limits. The browser host is out: at 25 spread players it would upload 0.5 MB/s, beyond many mobile uplinks, and relayed rooms stop at 15 joiners, so a 25-player world means a world-server world with `mostPlayers` and its select raised. On the authority side, 4.2 ms mean and 35.6 ms p99 per step leave little room once fights, invasions and a field battle land on the same thread, which is why the O(players × entities) passes must go first. The repository's planned "two welcomes at once" throttle for full-world downloads ([world_server_plan.md:1228](../generated/world_server_plan.md#L1228)) has no implementation in `server/`, and the 1.17 MB snapshots make it necessary. **Desktops will cope** at 9.1 ms per step mean (62.8 ms p99) and a 1.9 s first step. **Phones will not when players spread**, at roughly 45 ms of replay per 50 ms step before drawing anything. A 25-player lockstep world is therefore a desktop-first or "stay together" world, and an honest intermediate target is 12–16 players, gated on measuring a real phone.

The game's rules give out at 25 as well, because several of them are world singletons. Dungeons are shared, cleared for everyone, and only remade once *every* player has left and gone 120 m from the entrance ([DUNGEONS.md:842-845](../docs/DUNGEONS.md#L842-L845)), so two parties at the same door contest one master and one hoard. Per-party instances, which every themepark MMO uses to remove kill stealing, camping and ninja looting ([Wikipedia: Instance dungeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instance_dungeon)), are the first systems change. Rare encounters run one at a time per world with a 10-minute gap ([WILDS.md:860](../docs/WILDS.md#L860), [rare.js:52-66](../client/js/core/rare.js#L52-L66)), so the world serves at most about six an hour. Meanwhile each player's clock makes one due every 100–150 minutes in the wilds after the first ([WILDS.md:849-855](../docs/WILDS.md#L849-L855)), which means 25 players produce roughly 10–15 due an hour and most of them wait (arithmetic). A server world passes time only when *every* player rests ([host.js:445-452](../client/js/core/host.js#L445-L452)), any owner's "Pause for everyone" stops the world for all ([world-worker.js:9-15](../server/world-worker.js#L9-L15)), and the Players tab lists everyone with no paging ([partypanel.js:20-28](../client/js/app/partypanel.js#L20-L28)). Large PvE games have converged on everyone who contributes getting full personal credit and loot. Guild Wars 2 pays each participant "as if they were the only participant" ([GW2 Wiki: Event](https://wiki.guildwars2.com/wiki/Event)), and WoW lets up to five ungrouped players share a tag ([Warcraft Wiki: Multi-tap](https://warcraft.wiki.gg/wiki/Multi-tap)). Pellagos should adopt that rule, along with an area chat channel, friends, a report button that captures context, and a name filter. Trades between strangers need a two-sided confirmation and server-held item identities. Hosting at this tier costs nothing extra on the owner's Mac, since 4 Mbit/s fits a typical fixed-broadband uplink (the US median was 68 Mbit/s in Q1 2026, [IEEE ComSoc on Ookla](https://techblog.comsoc.org/2026/07/23/highlights-from-ooklas-u-s-speedtest-connectivity-report-mobile-fixed-networks/)), or **€36 a month** on a Hetzner CPX32 with 20 TB of traffic included ([Hetzner Cloud](https://www.hetzner.com/cloud/regular-performance/)).

### 50 players: replication becomes mandatory

At 50 spread players lockstep fails on every axis together. The authority's p99 is 68.5 ms and its worst step 111 ms, over the 50 ms budget without a single fight. A desktop copy spends 19.2 ms of each step replaying (38% of the main thread) and 3.4 s on its first step. Each client downloads 33 KB/s and the authority uploads 1.65 MB/s (13 Mbit/s). The welcome is 2.33 MB, encoded in 50 ms on the step thread. And the server-wide 64-player cap leaves no room for a second busy world. **This is where the model has to change.** The server keeps running the authoritative `Host` exactly as today, but instead of broadcasting ops it sends each client a view. That view announces actors entering and leaving the client's chunk neighbourhood, sends quantised binary deltas for those actors at up to 20 Hz through a priority accumulator, and carries a reliable event stream for swings, hits, deaths, loot, trades and chat. The client stops owning a whole world. It keeps a partial mirror of what is near it for the renderer and the HUD, predicts its own hero with the shared rules, draws everyone else about 100 ms behind, builds terrain and navmesh only round itself, and treats the server's state as truth, which retires the checksums and motion packets. The client was written against a complete local copy of the world, replayed each frame ([game.js:3727-3760](../client/js/app/game.js#L3727-L3760)), so every client system that reads world state has to learn to live with a partial one. **That is the largest single piece of work in this report.** On the server side, the players-times-entities passes move onto a grid, snapshot encoding and saves move off the step thread, and `LIMITS.players` becomes a setting.

The design changes at 50 are about shared spaces. Each player now has their own director deck ([WILDS.md:646-653](../docs/WILDS.md#L646-L653)), so many decks deal ambushes and rares into the same patch of land. Every shared-world game instead runs one spawner per place, scaled to the people present: most Guild Wars 2 events scale for up to 10 players ([GW2 Wiki: Event](https://wiki.guildwars2.com/wiki/Event)). An area director per region, with a budget that grows sublinearly with local players, keeps personal decks only for personal story cards. Contribution tiers (Guild Wars 2 pays 100%, 85% and 75% for gold, silver and bronze), a squad or raid group above the party of 4 for invasions and world bosses, guilds, and a market with a listing fee as a gold sink become necessary. Quest outcomes that change a town for everyone, such as a burned border village or a castle's order sworn after a boss, should either be phased per party or become cyclical shared states, the way Guild Wars 2's captured forts are retaken unless defended ([GW2 Wiki: Event](https://wiki.guildwars2.com/wiki/Event)). One box still serves the whole tier. At 20 KB/s a full world moves 2.6 TB a month, so a Hetzner CPX32 at €36 or an 8-core AX42 at **€79 with unmetered traffic** ([Hetzner AX](https://www.hetzner.com/dedicated-rootserver/matrix-ax/)) covers it.

### 100 players: one thread per world runs out

At 100 spread players lockstep is past every limit. The authority's mean step is 60.4 ms, so it is over budget on average, and its p99 is 273 ms with a worst step of 600 ms. A desktop copy needs 51.7 ms a step and 5.6 s to start, longer than the 5 s too-far limit. The authority uploads 5.66 MB/s (45 Mbit/s). The 4.50 MB snapshot exceeds the relay and broker's 4 MB message cap and takes 118 ms to encode, over the plan's 100 ms save budget ([world_server_plan.md:1528](../generated/world_server_plan.md#L1528)). And the server's 64-player cap refuses the 65th player outright. Even a hundred players together in one town upload 12.5–26 Mbit/s under lockstep. Replication (the 50 tier) removes the client and bandwidth problems. The authority's problem then depends on the per-player cost. If the grid fixes hold cost at the 0.15–0.17 ms per spread player measured at 8–25 players, 100 spread players would need about 15–17 ms a step on one thread, inside the budget with room for combat (arithmetic, to be confirmed by measurement). If they do not, or combat eats the headroom, the authority has to split. Dungeons and building insides go first: they are already separate maps, each door is a natural handoff, and each dungeon becomes a short-lived instance on its own worker thread, the same model New World uses for its session content ([AWS for Games](https://aws.amazon.com/blogs/gametech/the-unique-architecture-behind-amazon-games-seamless-mmo-new-world)). Overworld regions on two to four threads, with Albion-style handoff through the save, come only if the numbers demand them. Today every world's outbound text funnels through the supervisor's one event loop ([world-worker.js:101](../server/world-worker.js#L101), [play.js:340-342](../server/play.js#L340-L342)); at this tier workers should write to their own sockets. Persistence moves from whole-world snapshots to per-character records saved on events, with world state written behind in batches. That stops the save stall and makes trades atomic.

The client has to draw 100 players. The design that fits a phone is tiered. The nearest 8–16 players, plus the party and the current target, are drawn in full. The next 30–50 get simplified skinned meshes whose animation updates every few frames. Everyone else in view becomes a crowd figure: a shared, tinted body per people and sex, the technique Pellagos's townsfolk crowds already use. Distant players become impostors. GPU-drawn name plates appear for the nearest 20 or so, and a user-settable model limit with an automatic mode follows measured frame time, as in Guild Wars 2. Design at 100 means flagship bosses that scale up to 100 players, as Guild Wars 2's Shatterer does ([GW2 Wiki: Event](https://wiki.guildwars2.com/wiki/Event)). It means weekly and daily loot lockouts on bosses and rares, which WoW had to hotfix in after players farmed repeat kills ([Mein-MMO](https://mein-mmo.de/en/world-of-warcraft-weltbosse-tarlna-und-drov-gehotfixt-001-drop-p,24552)). It means caps with overflow copies for busy interiors (Guild Wars opened a new town district per 100 players, [Wikipedia: Instance dungeon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Instance_dungeon)), leashing so nobody can drag a pack onto another party, an admin panel for kicks, mutes and bans, opt-in PvP only, and economy tracking with a wealth Gini index and a price basket, as New World does ([New World: Economy Balance Deep Dive](https://www.newworld.com/en-gb/news/articles/economy-balance-deep-dive)). At 1.6 players per km² the wilds still feel empty outside towns, so public events must pull players together. Hosting at 100 costs **€79–159 a month** for an AX42 or a 16-core AX102 ([Hetzner AX](https://www.hetzner.com/dedicated-rootserver/matrix-ax/)). The same world under lockstep would push 14.7 TB a month, about $1,300 of AWS egress alone at $0.09/GB ([egresscost.com](https://egresscost.com/aws/)).

### 1,000 players: copies, not one bigger world

Under lockstep, 1,000 players is out by orders of magnitude. Extrapolating linearly from the measured slopes, which flatters the design, gives 220–450 KB/s into every phone, 1.8–3.7 Gbit/s of authority upload, a snapshot of tens of megabytes and steps of hundreds of milliseconds. Even fully replicated, a single world would need about 150 ms of authority work per 50 ms step at the best per-player cost measured, so at least three fully loaded threads and six to ten with headroom (arithmetic). The space is also too small socially. One thousand players on 64 km² is **15.6 per km²** on average, above the roughly 12 per km² of a 187-player Rust server on a 4 km map ([Rustalyzer](https://www.rustalyzer.com/server/1263009417)), and real play clusters in towns and at bosses.

There are two routes. The first is one partitioned world: region processes with border handoff, interest queries that cross borders, a gateway tier and global services. That is the New World, Albion and EVE route, and its failure points at scale are borders, transit and item consistency, as Star Citizen's 2026 elevator and lost-item problems show ([StarShipDealers summary](https://www.starshipdealers.com/blog/sc-live-tech-talk-server-meshing-2026/)). The second is about ten copies of a 100-player world, the Old School RuneScape and Guild Wars 2 model. Each copy is exactly the world the 100 tier built. A directory places players with their party, friends and guild, accounts, friends, chat, guilds and the market run as services above the copies, and quiet copies merge off-peak. **The second route reuses everything and adds no new simulation code.** It does bring its own rules. WoW Classic had to hotfix layer-hopping used to re-farm rares ([Blizzard Watch](https://blizzardwatch.com/2019/09/09/wow-classic-layer-hopping-abuse-hotfix/)) and held back world bosses until realms were down to one layer ([WoW Is Classic](https://www.wowisclassic.com/en/news/realm-layering-update/)), so Pellagos's once-per-world cards must become once per copy or once per party. Launches crowd the start town first, which is why FFXIV pre-planned instanced copies of new zones ([Siliconera](https://www.siliconera.com/ffxiv-dawntrail-launch-server-congestion-plans-detailed/)) and Throne and Liberty locked character creation on full servers ([Amazon Games](https://www.playthroneandliberty.com/news/articles/launch-prep-servers)). Operationally this tier adds a separate database host, staged deploys, alerting, a moderation queue and atomic character handoff between copies. On hardware, ten 100-player copies fit one 16-core AX102 with 128 GB on the arithmetic above, much as EVE packs about 13 single-threaded nodes onto a 16-core machine ([CCP](https://www.eveonline.com/news/view/tranquility-tech-iv)). At 20 KB/s each the copies move 160 Mbit/s, inside the box's unmetered 1 Gbit/s port.

## Hosting stays under €400 a month, even at 1,000

The cost table assumes a world full around the clock, which is an upper bound since average concurrency sits well below peak. It uses the measured lockstep upload at 25 players and 20 KB/s per player under replication above that.

| Tier | Assumed design | Sustained upload | Traffic a month | Cheapest sound option | Cost a month | AWS EC2 c8g.xlarge + egress |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 25 | Lockstep on the world server | 4 Mbit/s (measured) | 1.3 TB | Owner's Mac, or Hetzner CPX32 (4 vCPU, 20 TB included) | €0–36 | ≈$225 |
| 50 | Replication, one thread | 8 Mbit/s | 2.6 TB | Hetzner CPX32 or AX42 (8 Zen 4 cores, unmetered) | €36–79 (+€49 setup) | ≈$341 |

Traffic decides the gap. Hetzner's dedicated servers carry "unlimited and free" traffic on a guaranteed 1 Gbit/s port, at €59 for a 6-core AX41, €79–99 for an 8-core AX42 and €159–259 for a 16-core AX102 ([Hetzner AX](https://www.hetzner.com/dedicated-rootserver/matrix-ax/)). OVH's game servers start at $179 with game DDoS filtering and unlimited bandwidth ([OVHcloud](https://us.ovhcloud.com/bare-metal/game/)). AWS charges about $0.09/GB for internet egress ([beSharp](https://blog.besharp.it/aws-data-transfer-costs-in-a-nutshell/)), so egress alone exceeds compute at every tier, while a 4-vCPU c8g.xlarge costs about $117 a month on demand ([Vantage](https://instances.vantage.sh/aws/ec2/c8g.xlarge)). The one cheap AWS route is GameLift Servers, which has carried bandwidth free on sixth-generation and newer instances since 15 June 2026 ([AWS What's New](https://aws.amazon.com/it/about-aws/whats-new/2026/06/amazon-gamelift-servers-free-network-bandwidth/)). Prices are moving: Hetzner raised prices by up to 37% on 1 April 2026 ([heise](https://heise.de/-11185981)) and raised its CPX and CCX cloud plans 2–2.8× for new orders from 15 June ([wz-it](https://wz-it.com/en/blog/hetzner-price-increase-june-2026-cpx-ccx-alternatives/)), so pre-2026 hosting advice is out of date. Persistence is a small line: managed Postgres starts at about $15 a month ([InfraTally](https://infratally.com/articles/digitalocean-managed-postgres-deep-dive/)), or nothing extra on the same box until 1,000 players. Platforms priced per message or per call fit badly. A Cloudflare Durable Object has a soft limit of about 1,000 requests a second ([Cloudflare DO limits](https://developers.cloudflare.com/durable-objects/platform/limits)), far below 1,000 players' 20 Hz input. SpacetimeDB's metered Maincloud works out at roughly $1,220 a month for 100 players sending ten calls a second at list price ([SpacetimeDB pricing](https://spacetimedb.com/pricing)).

Risk and labour matter more than the server bill. Two of the best-known game hosts closed in 2026: Unity Multiplay on 31 March ([Unity docs](https://docs.unity.com/ugs/en-us/manual/game-server-hosting/manual/concepts/pricing-list)) and Hathora on 5 May, taking Stormgate's online play with it ([TechSpot](https://www.techspot.com/news/111969-stormgate-servers-go-dark-following-ai-focused-hosting.html)). The world server should stay a plain Node process in a portable container. Putting it behind Cloudflare's free WebSocket proxy, with the origin firewalled to Cloudflare's address ranges, gives DDoS protection at no cost and keeps the owner's home address out of strangers' hands. Legal duties start with chat, not with scale: the UK Online Safety Act has no size exemption for user-to-user services ([Bristows](https://www.bristows.com/content-hub/the-safety-net/scope-of-act/)), and the amended US COPPA rule has been enforced since 22 April 2026 ([Federal Register](https://www.federalregister.gov/documents/2025/04/22/2025-05904/childrens-online-privacy-protection-rule)). A public Pellagos world needs an age gate, a privacy policy, deletion on request and a reporting route from its first stranger. The indie record puts servers in proportion. Monsters & Memories spent just under $142,000 on development from 2020 to 2025, of which $18,875 (13.3%) went on servers ([Monsters & Memories: Development Costs](https://monstersandmemories.com/costs)). Hordes.io, a 3D browser MMORPG by one developer, shows community counts of about 1,300–1,450 online ([itch.io](https://dekdev.itch.io/hordesio), [MMOHuts](https://mmohuts.com/review/hordes-io)). Embers Adrift lives on about 20–30 concurrent players on Steam ([SteamCharts](https://steamcharts.com/app/3336530)). For a game this size, filling worlds is likelier to be the problem than capacity.

Stages 0–2 are cheap, carry over to either model, and answer the question this report could not: how a real phone fares with combat at 12, 16 and 25 players. Stage 3 is the point of no return and the main risk. Lockstep should stay the path for private, browser-hosted 8-player worlds until replication matches it in feel. After that, a browser host could serve views to up to 8 players with the same code, leaving one netcode path to maintain, or lockstep could be kept permanently for private play and maintained as a second path. Stage 5 can run in parallel with 3 and 4, since drawing crowds of players does not depend on how their positions arrive. Some of this work falls under the repository's own rules. A scale soak added to CI becomes a required check that only the owner can register ([contribution.md](../contribution.md), section 6), every script or workflow change belongs in `contribution.md`, and raising the cap means changing the unit test that pins it.

## Build for 100 per world, then copy worlds to reach 1,000

The strongest alternative is to stay a co-op game. Pellagos's systems were built for a small group: per-player director decks, one rare encounter per world, shared dungeons, a host who can pause, parties of 4. Its peers in that genre cap at 10–32. If the aim is the best co-op action RPG in a browser, Stages 0–2 deliver a 12–16-player world on the world server at almost no cost, and the work stops there. Optimising lockstep further does not change the conclusion, because no encoding trick removes the need for every phone to simulate every occupied region. Adopting a framework adds little: Colyseus offers rooms, per-client visibility and roughly 1,000–2,000 simple connections per vCPU ([Colyseus FAQ](https://docs.colyseus.io/faq)), but Pellagos's `Host` is the simulation and already runs in a server worker, so a framework would replace plumbing the game already has. Rewriting the server in Rust or Go throws away the shared rules that make own-hero prediction cheap. A single seamless 1,000-player world repeats the bet that sank SpatialOS's games and has kept Star Citizen's meshing in development for years.

The recommendation is a middle course. Run Stage 0 now, since it costs days and decides whether even 16 is safe on phones. Run Stages 1 and 2 next, since both a better co-op game and an MMO need them. Commit to Stages 3–6 only if shared worlds of strangers are the goal, and aim them at **50–100 players per world on one €79–159 server**. Add Stage 7 when, and only when, one 100-player world is regularly full. Ten such worlds behind shared services give 1,000 concurrent players for a few hundred euros a month without a line of new simulation code. The owner's scarce resource is time, not money, so the path should be the one where every stage leaves a playable game behind it.

## Conclusion

The audit turns the question around. The cap of 8 looks like a server limit, but the server copes with 25 players and with 100 who stay together. What a spread-out world exhausts first is every phone's main thread, which lockstep obliges to simulate the whole populated map. The way out is therefore less a bigger server than a different contract with the client: it simulates its own hero, renders what the server says is near it, and nothing else. The parts for that already exist in Pellagos (an authoritative worker per world, relevance radii, chunked terrain, crowd figures for townsfolk), which makes the move a large refactor of the client's relationship to the world rather than a new engine.

The second shift is that "1,000 players" is a product decision before it is a technical tier. An 8 km world cannot hold a thousand people socially, at a density above a full Rust server's, and every game that has reached that number did so with copies, queues and merges rather than one unlimited space. The live risk for a solo developer is not that ten 100-player worlds will be too expensive, since they cost less than a single month of AWS egress for one 100-player lockstep world. The risk is that one 100-player world never fills. Building for a full world of 100, with design that pulls players together, is the bet worth making first.
